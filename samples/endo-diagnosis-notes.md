# Pulpal and Periapical Diagnosis

## Pulp testing
Cold testing is the most common thermal test for pulp sensibility.
A refrigerant spray is applied on a cotton pellet to the middle third of the buccal surface.
The electric pulp test (EPT) shows whether pulp nerve fibers can respond; it does not measure blood supply.
EPT can give false negatives in recently traumatized teeth and in teeth with immature apices.
A-delta fibers are myelinated nerve fibers that carry sharp, well-localized pain.
C fibers are unmyelinated nerve fibers that carry dull, aching, poorly localized pain.
Always test adjacent and contralateral teeth first so the patient knows what a normal response feels like.

## Pulpal diagnoses
- Normal pulp: symptom-free and responds normally to pulp testing.
- Reversible pulpitis: inflammation that should resolve once the cause is removed; cold causes sharp pain that fades within a few seconds.
- Symptomatic irreversible pulpitis: lingering thermal pain, spontaneous pain and referred pain; the pulp cannot heal.
- Asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis: no symptoms, but deep caries or trauma means the pulp will not heal.
- Pulp necrosis: death of the dental pulp; the tooth does not respond to pulp testing.

Lingering pain is usually defined as pain lasting more than 30 seconds after the stimulus is removed.
Treatment for irreversible pulpitis is root canal therapy or extraction.

## Apical diagnoses
- Normal apical tissues: not sensitive to percussion or palpation, with an intact lamina dura and a uniform PDL space.
- Symptomatic apical periodontitis: inflammation of the apical periodontium that causes pain on biting, percussion or palpation.
- Asymptomatic apical periodontitis: an apical radiolucency without clinical symptoms.
- Acute apical abscess: rapid onset, spontaneous pain, tenderness to pressure, pus formation and swelling.
- Chronic apical abscess: gradual onset with little or no discomfort and intermittent drainage through a sinus tract.
- Condensing osteitis: a diffuse radiopaque lesion at the apex that reflects a bony reaction to low-grade inflammation.

Percussion tests the periodontal ligament, not the pulp.
A sinus tract should be traced with a gutta-percha cone on a radiograph to find its source.

## Quick numbers
The primary dentition has 20 teeth and the permanent dentition has 32.
Root formation is usually complete about three years after a permanent tooth erupts.
